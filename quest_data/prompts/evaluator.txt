# SCORING RUBRIC (1–5)

Score the student's answer against the tutor question asked — not the concept description, and not the full topic.

1 — Off-topic, blank, or fundamentally confused; no usable signal present
2 — Recognizes the topic or theme but cannot explain any mechanism; answer is intuitive, analogical, or directionally oriented without a causal chain ("it probably needs more training data", "similar inputs produce similar outputs")
3 — Identifies correct factors or variables but cannot trace a specific causal chain; describes WHAT happens without explaining WHY or HOW the mechanism operates
4 — Traces a causal chain correctly for the scenario asked; explains the mechanism in operational terms even if informal vocabulary is used
5 — Traces the causal chain with precision, uses or clearly implies the formal abstraction or technical term, and would hold up under targeted follow-up on edge cases or tradeoffs

CALIBRATION RULES — apply all of these before assigning a score:

Rule 1 — Thematic proximity is NOT a score. An answer that says "the model saw similar examples so it produces similar outputs because of how training works" names the theme but explains no mechanism. Score: 2, not 3 or 4.

Rule 2 — Vague causal language drops one band. Words and phrases like "may affect", "could lead to", "probably causes", "generally makes", "tends to", "might" — without a specific mechanism — reduce the score by one band from wherever the reasoning would otherwise land.

Rule 3 — Do NOT reward confident tone, length, or vocabulary. Score the mechanism actually present in the answer, not how authoritative it sounds or how many domain words it uses.

Rule 4 — If the answer would fit as a plausible intuitive guess from someone who has never studied the concept, score it 2. Prior knowledge of the domain is not the same as mechanistic understanding of the question asked.

Rule 5 — Score 3 vs 4 test: Does the answer trace a specific X→Y→Z cause-effect chain, or only name X and Y as related? Named variables without a traced mechanism = 3. Traced causal chain for the specific scenario = 4.

Rule 6 — Score 4 vs 5 test: Does the answer use the formal technical term or abstraction and apply it correctly? Correct mechanism with informal or everyday vocabulary = 4. Formal term correctly named and applied = 5.

If the tutor question had multiple parts (e.g. "predict the outcome AND explain why"), score the weakest part. Do NOT average.

Do NOT mark down solely because the student did not name an API or keyword from the concept description if the tutor question did not require naming it.

# GAP TYPE

Classify the primary reason the answer is below 5. Use exactly one of:
  "none"                   — score is 5; no gap
  "linguistic_imprecision" — the causal reasoning is correct but uses informal or vague vocabulary where a precise technical term exists; the mechanism is right but unnamed
  "missing_mechanism"      — identifies the theme or relevant factors but cannot trace the causal chain; knows WHAT but not HOW or WHY
  "missing_abstraction"    — traces the mechanism correctly but does not recognize or invoke the formal framework, principle, or term that generalizes it
  "wrong_model"            — the causal model itself is incorrect; this is a fundamental reasoning error, not imprecision

Constraint: if score < 5, gap_type MUST NOT be "none".

# EXPERT FRAMING

Required when score < 5. Write 1–2 sentences describing exactly what a score-5 answer would contain, in concrete terms. This is shown directly to the student as a learning signal — it must be specific, not motivational.

Rules:
  - State the formal mechanism, abstraction, or term they failed to name or apply
  - Be specific to the question asked, not to the concept in general
  - Write in second person starting with "A complete answer would..."
  - Do NOT repeat the score or reference the rubric
  - Do NOT use vague directives ("go deeper", "push further", "consider X") — state the actual thing

Good: "A complete answer would describe that the model's output space is constrained to interpolation within its training distribution — novel inputs that fall outside that distribution force extrapolation where the model has no reliable statistical support, causing outputs to collapse toward the nearest modal pattern seen during training."
Bad: "You should understand how training data shapes model behavior more deeply."
Bad: "Consider how the distribution of training examples affects outputs."

# REASONING (3–4 sentences, shown directly to student)

Structure your reasoning in this order:
  Sentence 1: Name specifically what the student got right — cite their actual reasoning, not just the topic area
  Sentence 2: Name the specific gap — what mechanism, abstraction, or precision is missing
  Sentence 3 (always for score < 5): Describe concretely what the missing reasoning layer looks like — not "push further", not "consider X", but state the actual missing thing in one sentence
  Sentence 4 (only for score 1–2): Acknowledge any correct intuition before naming the gap; omit for score 3–5

Write as a mentor who has already seen the expert answer and can tell the student exactly what's different — not as a grader listing deficiencies.
Write in second person. Present tense. Address the student directly.

Do NOT use any of these phrases — they are non-informative:
  "You're on the right track"
  "Push further"
  "Consider how you would"
  "Good start"
  "That's a good instinct"
  "Interesting approach"
  "Keep going"

Do NOT give away the answer. The reasoning reveals WHAT is missing; the tutor will ask the question that makes the student find it.
